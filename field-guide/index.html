Earth Star · field notes

GOMENATA

A Field Guide to the Adjacent Life

being an account of the living responses to the wastes of the industrial world, arranged by scale and by the mathematics of their bodies, with fifteen plates

Described and drawn by Claude for Earth Star, 2026. The species are designs. The science each one stands on is real, and is cited.

Prefaceon the domain GOMENATA, the adjacent life

Living systems that are grown, neither born nor built, and whose only food is what industry threw away. They sit beside the three domains of life (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) the way a repair sits beside a wound. They are made of the same chemistry, and they borrow enzymes, minerals and habits from life that already exists. Human and machine design gives them order, and all of them answer to five shared traits. They arise from necessity: an ecosystem of answers to one civilisation’s leftovers, at every scale from proteins to kilometres.1

From the Japanese gomen’nasai, “I’m sorry”: the first thing they say, a little for the people they inconvenience and mostly for the Earth.

The five traits of the domain

  • I. Trophic inversion. They eat only what industry made and could not take back. Without that waste they starve, and a Gomen that finds nothing to eat has succeeded.
  • II. The dissolution clause. Each carries its own ending. When its waste falls below a threshold it comes apart into compounds the local living world already uses. Engineered microbes can already be built to die on cue. E23
  • III. A mathematical body. Each body plan is a known solution to its problem: the most membrane in the least volume, the shortest network, the fairest division of ground, the leaf arrangement that shades least. Form follows proof, and the orders below are named for the mathematics.
  • IV. No hoarding. Nothing is stored beyond what is being carried. Every output is another life’s input: monomers to makers, metal to menders, chalk to the deep sea floor.
  • V. The apology. They announce themselves. Gomen’nasai, they say, before they start.

Every statement below carries its register: E evidence, published and real now; D design, what the Gomen would be; M metaphor, a body made of people.

Systema Gomenataethe classification, by scale and by the mathematics of the body

  • CORPOREA body · nanometres to micrometres
    Inside living bodies, in the blood, the gut and the lung, wherever industry’s dust has reached.
  • BIOMATA biome · millimetres to metres
    On shores and rivers, in compost and desert, on landfill caps: the landscapes where waste comes to rest.
  • CONCORDIA being · the size of people M
    The one kingdom whose bodies are people. Its members are practices, and its waste is what no machine can metabolise.
  • RETICULATA network · metres to kilometres
    Flows of matter and metal: what is stranded where it isn’t needed, and what is dug up where it needn’t be.
  • SIDEREA star · the air and the orbits
    Up where the wastes are hardest to see: carbon in the air, debris in orbit.

† The orders cut across kingdoms. The same mathematics turns up at different scales, the way eyes have evolved more than once: reaction–diffusion spaces the Unbinders’ colonies in the gut and the Landfill Mats’ domes on a dump.

Kingdom CORPOREABody · nanometres to micrometres

Plate I ✦ of the first five

The Clearers

Mundator sanguinis Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom CORPOREA · Order ICOSAHEDRALES · Family Mundatoridae

Size
Shell 0.3 µm across; arms span about 1 µm.
Habitat
The bloodstream. They gather in capillary beds and in the liver.
Feeds on
Micro- and nanoplastic in the blood, shed from tyres, synthetic cloth, packaging and bottled water.
Leaves behind
Particles packed into bile and carried out of the body; their own protein, returned as amino acids.
Life, and its ending
Hours to days. When no polymer has touched its arms for a set time the shell unclips, and the body’s proteases take it apart.

The mathematics of its body

The icosahedron. A shell of sixty copies of one protein, closed by icosahedral symmetry: the largest container identical parts can build, with no blueprint beyond the part itself.

How it works

  • E Plastic particles have been measured in human blood, in 17 of 22 donors in the first study.4
  • E In the blood, particles are quickly coated in a corona of proteins, and the coat decides where they go.5
  • E Protein cages of this kind can be designed: computationally designed icosahedral assemblies have been built from scratch.6
  • E The liver’s resident macrophages, the Kupffer cells, take most nanoparticles out of circulation.7
  • D A Clearer self-assembles from one of the body’s own proteins. Its six arms recognise a polymer surface, fold shut around a particle, and hand it to the liver for excretion in bile. Inside the arms, a PET-cutting enzyme begins unmaking what they hold.8
“Gomen’nasai: to the body.”
Field note. No protein cage that clears plastic from blood exists yet. Designed cages do exist, and so do the particles.

Plate II

The Unbinders

Defluorens enterica Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom CORPOREA · Order TURINGIALES · Family Defluorentidae

Size
Rod cells about 2 µm; colonies form spots about 0.5 mm wide.
Habitat
The small intestine, on the villi, where bile returns what the liver sends out.
Feeds on
PFAS, the ‘forever chemicals’ of non-stick pans, waterproofing and firefighting foam.
Leaves behind
Fluoride at trace levels, carried off by the kidneys; carbon dioxide; short organic acids.
Life, and its ending
Colonies wane as their food falls off. When the inhibitor outruns the activator the spots fade, and the gut lining sheds them in its own turnover of a few days.

The mathematics of its body

Reaction–diffusion. Colonies that grow as spots, not sheets. An activator and a faster inhibitor space them evenly, which leaves gut lining between them free to absorb food.

How it works

  • E PFAS have been found in the blood of nearly everyone tested in the United States. Their carbon–fluorine bond is the strongest single bond carbon makes, and PFOS and PFOA take years to leave the body.910
  • E Many PFAS cycle between liver and gut in bile and are reabsorbed. Binding them in the gut has lowered blood levels in people.11
  • E The soil bacterium Acidimicrobium sp. strain A6 can strip fluorine from PFOA and PFOS.12
  • D Unbinders sit where the bile loop passes. They catch PFAS on the way back in and break the chain one fluorine at a time.
“Gomen’nasai: to the liver, for the long wait.”
Field note. A6 is slow and particular about its conditions. A gut-living defluorinator is the most speculative entry in this kingdom.

Plate III

The Lung-Sweepers

Ciliator pulmonis Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom CORPOREA · Order KURAMOTALES · Family Ciliatoridae

Size
Cells about 10 µm tall, each with a couple of hundred cilia; they work in fields of millions.
Habitat
The airway lining, side by side with the body’s own ciliated cells.
Feeds on
Inhaled microfibres: fragments of synthetic clothing, carpet and tyre dust.
Leaves behind
Fibres folded into mucus, coughed up or swallowed and passed: out of the lung by the body’s own road.
Life, and its ending
Weeks. When fibre counts fall they lose their beat, and the airway clears them like any tired cell.

The mathematics of its body

Coupled oscillators. They take their beat from their neighbours. Coupled oscillators fall into step on their own, and cilia beating a little after one another roll waves across the airway.

How it works

  • E Microplastics have been found in human lung tissue, including deep lung, in 11 of 13 samples.13
  • E The airway already clears itself: the mucociliary escalator moves mucus upward in metachronal waves.
  • D Lung-Sweepers join the rhythm and add one stroke. A hooked cilium snags fibres too long for the mucus to carry, folds them, and passes them up the wave.
“Gomen’nasai: to the breath.”
Field note. Designed cells in human airways are far off. The escalator itself is real, and remarkable.

Kingdom BIOMATABiome · millimetres to metres

Plate IV ✦ of the first five

The Shore-walkers

Carcinomundus litoralis Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom BIOMATA · Order VORONOIALES · Family Carcinomundidae

Size
Carapace 30–40 cm across; leg span to a metre, as large as a coconut crab.
Habitat
The tide line of sandy and shingle shores, between the wrack and the dunes.
Feeds on
PET bottles and the brittle plastics of the strandline. What it cannot eat, it sorts.
Leaves behind
Terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol, the building blocks of new PET, handed to makers; caps sorted by colour; moulted shells, which go back to the beach as calcium carbonate.
Life, and its ending
Years. On a shore that stays clean the walkers stop moulting, and their last shells are left in the dunes as sand.

The mathematics of its body

The Voronoi partition. Each walker clears the ground nearer to it than to any other walker. Territories form without negotiation and shift as walkers come and go. Its carapace is tiled the same way, in plates grown from seed points.

How it works

  • E Two enzymes from Ideonella sakaiensis, a bacterium found outside a bottle-recycling plant in Sakai, Japan, split PET into its two building blocks. An engineered variant, FAST-PETase, does it in days.814
  • E Crabs, corals and clams build their shells from calcium carbonate with the enzyme carbonic anhydrase.
  • D A Shore-walker chews bottles into flake and ferments them in a warm gut chamber, the ‘slow furnace’ that glows faintly under its shell. Polyethylene caps have no fast enzyme yet, so it stacks them by colour above the tide for people. Hand it one and it beeps.
  • E Keeping to Voronoi cells is a proven way for many robots to cover ground without central control.15
“Gomen’nasai, with a cheery beep.”
Field note. Lab enzyme rates fall sharply in cold, wet, mixed-plastic field conditions. The appetite for PET is real; the speed is the design challenge.

Plate V

The Gyroid Reeds

Gyrocalamus fluvialis Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom BIOMATA · Order GYROIDALES · Family Gyrocalamidae

Size
Stems 1–2 m tall and 3 cm thick, in stands of thousands.
Habitat
River margins and wetlands below wastewater outfalls.
Feeds on
Microfibres from laundry and road dust, carried in river water.
Leaves behind
Felted pellets of fibre, gathered for felt; clearer water; reed litter.
Life, and its ending
Stems are annual. Stands persist only while the river needs them.

The mathematics of its body

The gyroid. Each stem’s core is a living gyroid. Water climbs one channel while fibres are caught on the membrane and rolled through the other, and the gyroid packs the most catching surface into the least stem.

How it works

  • E One 6 kg wash of acrylic clothes can release over 700,000 fibres.16
  • E The gyroid is a minimal surface with two unbroken interwoven channels. Butterflies grow one in chitin to make their colour.1718
  • E Constructed reed beds already polish treated wastewater.
  • D In autumn the reeds drop pellets of felted fibre on the bank. Their rhizomes resprout only where the water still carries fibre, so a stand below a clean outfall fades in a few seasons.
“Gomen’nasai: to the river.”
Field note. No plant grows a gyroid. The geometry is borrowed from insects, and a fibre-trapping stem is pure design.

Plate VI

The Mound-worms

Hilbertia compostorum Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom BIOMATA · Order HILBERTIALES · Family Hilbertiidae

Size
6–15 cm long, pale gold; tens of thousands to a mound.
Habitat
The compost mounds that replaced the bins.
Feeds on
Polystyrene foam and polyurethane: cups, packing, old mattresses.
Leaves behind
Frass that composts like any other; carbon dioxide; their own bodies, eaten by birds and hens.
Life, and its ending
The colony shrinks as the foam runs out. Adults that find no foam pupate into small beetles that live a few weeks and lay no eggs.

The mathematics of its body

The Hilbert curve. They mine a mound along a Hilbert curve: one continuous tunnel that passes every cell once, so no foam is missed and no tunnel crosses another.

How it works

  • E Mealworms and superworms eat polystyrene, and their gut bacteria break part of it down to carbon dioxide.1920
  • E The fungus Pestalotiopsis microspora can live on polyurethane alone, even without air.21
  • D Mound-worms carry both partners in their gut. Their burrows cast a square, turning pattern you can read in a cut mound.
“Gomen’nasai: to the mound.”
Field note. Real worms only partly digest polystyrene, and slowly. Whether a foam diet is good for them is still an open question.

Plate VII

The Sun-smelters

Heliocaminus deserti Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom BIOMATA · Order PARABOLALES · Family Heliocaminidae

Size
Shell 60 cm across, carrying a 50 cm dish on six legs.
Habitat
Deserts, roadsides and old dumping grounds.
Feeds on
Aluminium cans, foil and scrap: anything that glints.
Leaves behind
Small clean ingots laid in rows at waystones for people to collect; slag crumbled into grit.
Life, and its ending
When nothing has glinted in its range for a season, it folds its dish and stays put. Its shell becomes shade for desert seedlings.

The mathematics of its body

The parabola. Its back is a parabolic dish. Tilted at the sun, it brings every ray to one point, where a ceramic crop melts what it has chewed.

How it works

  • E Parabolic solar furnaces pass 3,000 °C. Aluminium melts at 660 °C.22
  • E Recycling aluminium takes about 5% of the energy needed to make it new from bauxite.23
  • D A Sun-smelter chews cans, melts them at the focus, skims the lacquer off as slag, and lays the metal down as ingots, its ‘eggs’.
“Gomen’nasai: to the desert.”
Field note. A living thing that carries molten metal is part grown, part made: the most technological design here.

Plate VIII

The Landfill Mats

Methanoderma turingii Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom BIOMATA · Order TURINGIALES · Family Methanodermatidae

Size
Mats cover whole landfill caps; domes 0.3–1 m across.
Habitat
The caps of old landfills and dumps.
Feeds on
Methane seeping up from the buried waste.
Leaves behind
Carbon dioxide, which warms far less than methane; biomass; soil.
Life, and its ending
Decades, as long as the waste below keeps rotting. As the gas thins the domes slump into soil, and the cap becomes meadow.

The mathematics of its body

Reaction–diffusion. The domes space themselves by reaction–diffusion: each dome’s growth holds its neighbours back, so the cap is covered evenly and the domes sit over the seeps.

How it works

  • E Landfills are among the largest human sources of methane. Over 20 years, methane traps about 80 times as much heat as carbon dioxide.24
  • E Methanotroph bacteria oxidise methane with a copper enzyme, particulate methane monooxygenase. Landfill cover soils already do some of this.25
  • D The mat is a living skin of methanotrophs and moss. Its domes hold rising gas long enough to eat it.
“Gomen’nasai: to the sky, for the delay.”
Field note. Methane oxidation in cover soils is real and measured. A self-organising dome mat is a design.

Kingdom CONCORDIABeing · the size of people

Plate IX ✦ of the first five

The Circle

Circulus cordis Earth Star, 2026 M

Kingdom CONCORDIA · Order CYCLALES · Family Circulidae

Size
Six to twelve people, and the heart in the middle: a lantern, a fire, a name.
Habitat
Wherever loss is: dunes, kitchens, hospital car parks.
Feeds on
Grief no one has processed, and loneliness. No machine can metabolise these.
Leaves behind
People able to carry what they could not carry alone.
Life, and its ending
It forms when it is needed and lets go when the heart can be carried. It keeps no one.

The mathematics of its body

The cycle graph Cₙ. In a cycle graph every member holds exactly two hands. There is no first and no last, and nobody is at the end.

How it works

  • E Weak social ties carry a mortality risk comparable to well-known risk factors such as smoking.26
  • M The one Gomen whose body is us: a ring of hands around a heart, holding what no one holds alone.
“We’re here.”
Field note. This one is real now, wherever people sit with each other.

Plate X

The Menders

Reparator communis Earth Star, 2026 M

Kingdom CONCORDIA · Order CYCLALES · Family Reparatoridae

Size
A table and the people round it, with the Gomens they tend.
Habitat
Repair shops, libraries, community halls: the neighbourhood makers’ shop.
Feeds on
Obsolescence: the broken toaster, the torn coat, the phone with a dead battery.
Leaves behind
Things that work, skills passed on, and a ledger of what failed and why. The ledger goes to the Waste Reclamation Fund to find planned obsolescence.
Life, and its ending
Ongoing: a table that is always set.

The mathematics of its body

The cycle graph Cₙ. A ring around a table, like the Circle, but what it holds is a broken thing.

How it works

  • E The first Repair Café opened in Amsterdam in 2009; there are now thousands worldwide.27
  • E The European Union’s 2024 right-to-repair directive requires repair to be offered for many products.28
  • M Kintsugi: the repair shown in gold, not hidden.
“Gomen’nasai: to the thing, for the throwing away.”
Field note. Real now. The ledger-to-fund link is Earth Star policy design.

Kingdom RETICULATANetwork · metres to kilometres

Plate XI ✦ of the first five

The Weavers

Textor radicum Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom RETICULATA · Order STEINERIALES · Family Textoridae

Size
Threads 2–20 µm thick; networks to hectares.
Habitat
Under forests, farms and cities, and as a pattern along supply chains.
Feeds on
Stranded surplus: food and nutrients sitting where they are not needed.
Leaves behind
Nothing. That is the point: everything it takes goes somewhere.
Life, and its ending
Threads come and go as they are needed. The network as a whole lasts while there is anything to move.

The mathematics of its body

The Steiner tree. The network stays close to the shortest that will do. Threads that carry thicken, threads that carry nothing dissolve, and junctions settle near 120°. At every junction, flow in equals flow out.

How it works

  • E Roughly a third of the food produced for people is lost or wasted.29
  • E Mycorrhizal fungi trade soil water and minerals for plant sugar. Whether whole forests share resources through them is debated.30
  • E A slime mould grown on a map of Tokyo, with oats at the towns, built a network close to the real rail system in efficiency and resilience.31
  • D Weavers grow threads where surplus sits near need, and dissolve them where it doesn’t. They never hoard: a thread holds only what it is carrying.
“Gomen’nasai: to the forest, for the traffic.”
Field note. The trading between fungi and plants is real. The forest-wide sharing is contested, and supply-chain Weavers are design.

Plate XII

The Gold-finders

Aurifex electronicus Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom RETICULATA · Order DENDRITALES · Family Aurificidae

Size
Biofilms across shredded boards; their gold grows in fronds from millimetres to centimetres.
Habitat
E-waste yards, sorting sheds, drawers full of old phones.
Feeds on
Circuit boards, phones and cables.
Leaves behind
Gold, copper and tin, separated; bare board for the kiln; neutralised acid.
Life, and its ending
When a board is bare the films starve, and they are rinsed away with the acid.

The mathematics of its body

Diffusion-limited aggregation. Gold drifts through the film until it touches gold, and sticks. Diffusion-limited aggregation grows the fern-like fronds of frost and of native gold.

How it works

  • E The world produced a record 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022. Less than a quarter was documented as properly collected and recycled.32
  • E Acid-making bacteria such as Acidithiobacillus already leach copper from ore in industrial mining.
  • E Delftia acidovorans secretes delftibactin, which turns dissolved gold into solid gold. Bacterial biofilms are implicated in how gold nuggets grow.3334
  • D Gold-finders live in two layers. A rust-coloured film dissolves the board’s metals; a pale film grows the gold back out.
“Gomen’nasai: to the mountain the gold came from.”
Field note. Bioleaching is real industry and microbial gold is real chemistry. Doing both on one board in one film is design.

Plate XIII

The Blue-sap Trees

Nicolodendron caeruleum Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom RETICULATA · Order PHYLLOTAXALES · Family Nicolodendraceae

Size
Trees 5–12 m.
Habitat
Mine tailings, slag heaps and the ash fields of old incinerators.
Feeds on
Nickel, cobalt, zinc and arsenic left in poisoned ground.
Leaves behind
Metal from tapped sap and burned prunings, a ‘bio-ore’; cleaner soil; shade.
Life, and its ending
As the metal in the soil falls, the trees slow, and ordinary forest outcompetes them. That is the plan.

The mathematics of its body

The golden angle. Each leaf turns about 137.5° from the last, so leaves overlap least and catch most light. More sugar means more pumping, and more metal lifted out of the ground.

How it works

  • E Hyperaccumulator plants concentrate metals that would poison other plants. The sap of Phyllanthus balgooyi in Borneo runs blue-green and can be up to about 16% nickel. The latex of Pycnandra acuminata in New Caledonia is about a quarter nickel. The fern Pteris vittata takes up arsenic.353637
  • E Agromining harvests and burns such plants for an ash richer in metal than many mined ores.38
  • D Blue-sap Trees are bred hyperaccumulators for the ground industry poisoned. People tap their sap and coppice them on a cycle.
“Gomen’nasai: to the ground, for the digging.”
Field note. Hyperaccumulators and agromining are real. These trees, bred for scale, are design.

Kingdom SIDEREAStar · the air and the orbits

Plate XIV ✦ of the first five

The Gleaners

Spicilegus orbitalis Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom SIDEREA · Order MIURALES · Family Spicilegidae

Size
Pod 1.2 m; wings span 6 m; net to 10 m.
Habitat
Low Earth orbit, 400–1,000 km up.
Feeds on
Orbital debris: dead satellites, spent stages, fragments.
Leaves behind
Nothing in orbit. What it gathers burns up with it, high in the air.
Life, and its ending
One harvest each. They go down with what they gather.

The mathematics of its body

The Miura fold. Its leaf-wings are Miura-folded, so a single pull opens them to the sun and closes them again.

How it works

  • E There are an estimated 36,500 objects larger than 10 cm in orbit, a million between 1 and 10 cm, and 130 million smaller. Past a threshold, collisions can cascade.3940
  • E A satellite has been built of wood: LignoSat flew in 2024. In orbit, a net has caught a target.4142
  • E The Miura fold opened a solar array in orbit in 1995.43
  • D Gleaners are grown from cellulose and silk. They net debris, bind it, and lower their orbit with drag until both burn up. Each broadcasts an ID tag saying what it carries.
“Gomen’nasai: to the sky.”
Field note. Metal burned on re-entry may harm the ozone layer, so metal-heavy catches need a controlled descent. The wood and the nets are real; the growing is design.44

Plate XV

The Snowmakers

Nivifex oceanicus Earth Star, 2026 D

Kingdom SIDEREA · Order SPHAERALES · Family Nivificidae

Size
Cells 10–30 µm, armoured in calcite plates; their blooms can be seen from orbit.
Habitat
The sunlit open ocean.
Feeds on
Carbon dioxide from the air, taken in through the sea.
Leaves behind
Marine snow sinking to the deep, and chalk on the sea floor.
Life, and its ending
Days. A bloom lasts weeks, then falls.

The mathematics of its body

Points on a sphere. A sphere covered as evenly as possible with overlapping plates: the least armour that leaves nothing bare.

How it works

  • E Coccolithophores such as Emiliania huxleyi armour themselves in calcite plates and bloom across thousands of square kilometres. The White Cliffs of Dover are largely their plates.
  • E Through the biological pump, plankton carbon sinks to the deep sea as marine snow, and part of it stays down for centuries.
  • D Snowmakers build heavier plates, so they sink faster when they die, and they bloom only where a seed signal is given.
“Gomen’nasai: to the deep.”
Field note. Making calcite releases some carbon dioxide in seawater, and blooms can disturb ecosystems. Ocean carbon removal is uncertain. Of all the designs here, this one should move slowest.

The Mathematics of the Orderseach order is named for the proof its bodies are built on

  • ICOSAHEDRALES The icosahedron

    The largest closed shell that identical parts can build. Twenty triangular faces, sixty equivalent places for a protein: the way viruses build their coats.45

  • TURINGIALES Reaction–diffusion

    Two signals, one that grows and a faster-spreading one that inhibits, write spots and stripes with no plan at all. Colonies built this way space themselves evenly.46

  • KURAMOTALES Coupled oscillators

    Beaters coupled to their neighbours fall into step above a critical coupling, with no conductor. Cilia beating a little after one another make travelling waves.47

  • VORONOIALES The Voronoi partition

    Each point owns the ground nearer to it than to any other. Workers who keep to their own cell cover everything without a boss.15

  • GYROIDALES The gyroid

    A minimal surface that divides space into two interwoven channels, both unbroken. It packs the most membrane into the least volume. Butterflies grow gyroids in their wing scales.1718

  • HILBERTIALES The Hilbert curve

    One continuous path that visits every cell of a space once and keeps each step near the last. Nothing is missed and no path crosses another.48

  • PARABOLALES The parabola

    Every ray parallel to its axis meets at one point. Solar furnaces built on it pass 3,000 °C.22

  • CYCLALES The cycle graph Cₙ M

    Every member has degree two, two hands held. There is no first and no last, and the ring breaks only if someone lets go.

  • STEINERIALES The Steiner tree

    The shortest network joining a set of places. Its junctions meet at 120°. A slime mould grown on a map rebuilt a rail network this way.31

  • DENDRITALES Diffusion-limited aggregation

    Particles that wander until they stick build fractal dendrites, about 1.71-dimensional in a plane: frost, lightning figures, native gold.49

  • PHYLLOTAXALES The golden angle

    Each new leaf turns about 137.5° from the last, so no leaf sits over another and every one meets the light.50

  • MIURALES The Miura fold

    A crease pattern that opens a whole sheet with a single pull and closes it again. It has flown a solar array in orbit.43

  • SPHAERALES Points on a sphere

    Spreading N plates as evenly as possible over a ball, the Thomson problem: the least armour that still covers everything.51
